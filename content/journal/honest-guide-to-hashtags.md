Hashtags are one of the most over-thought and under-used tools in local marketing. People either ignore them completely or paste thirty random ones under every post like a magic spell. The truth sits in between, and it’s refreshingly simple.

## What a hashtag actually does

A hashtag is a filing label. It sorts your post into a topic or place so people browsing that topic can find it. That’s it. It won’t rescue a boring post, and it won’t magically make you go viral. But used sensibly, it helps the *right* local people stumble across you — and for a local business, that’s the whole game.

## The mistakes that make you look amateur

- **The wall of thirty tags.** It reads as desperate and spammy, and most are irrelevant anyway.
- **Tags that are too big.** #food has hundreds of millions of posts; yours is gone in a second. You’re a minnow in an ocean.
- **Made-up tags nobody searches.** #WeDoGreatWork sorts your post into a folder of one.
- **The same copy-pasted block every time.** Platforms notice, and it does you no favours.

## What actually works: think local and specific

For a Gloucestershire business, the useful tags are the ones a nearby customer might actually browse:

- **Place tags:** #Cheltenham #Stroud #Cotswolds #Gloucestershire #Cirencester
- **Niche-plus-place:** #CheltenhamFlorist #StroudBarber #CotswoldWedding
- **Topic tags of a sensible size** — big enough to have an audience, small enough that you’re not instantly buried

A smaller, specific tag with ten thousand posts is far more useful to you than a giant one with ten million. You want to be a big fish in a small, local pond.

## How many, and where

Five to ten relevant tags is plenty. Beyond that you get diminishing returns and start to look like you’re gaming it. Put them at the end of the caption, or in the first comment if you’d rather keep the caption clean — both work fine.

## The honest bit

Hashtags are a nudge, not an engine. They help a good post reach a few more of the right people. They do nothing for a post that wasn’t worth seeing. So get the photo and the words right first — then add a handful of specific, local tags to give it a gentle push.

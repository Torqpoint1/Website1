It’s the time of year when everyone starts building elaborate content calendars — colour-coded spreadsheets with a post slotted into every single day of 2027. They look impressive in November and are abandoned by February. The plan you’ll actually stick to is far simpler. It fits on one page.

## Why the giant plan fails

A forty-tab calendar assumes you’ll know in January exactly what you’ll want to say in July. You won’t — and that’s fine, because the point of a plan isn’t to script every post. It’s to give you *direction*, so you never again stare at a blank screen wondering what to post. Direction fits on a page. Scripts don’t survive contact with a real year.

## The one-page plan

Four short sections. That’s the whole thing.

**1. The goal (one line).** What is marketing actually *for* next year? More enquiries for a specific service? Filling the quiet season? Building the email list? Pick one main thing. A plan aimed at everything aims at nothing.

**2. Three or four themes.** The handful of topics you’ll keep returning to all year — the things you want to be known for. For a bathroom fitter: finished jobs, honest advice, the local area. For a café: the food, the people, what’s on this season. Everything you post hangs off one of these.

**3. A realistic rhythm.** Not "daily". What can you *actually* sustain? Two posts a week, one email a month, your Google profile kept current. Write down the cadence you can keep in a busy month, not a quiet one.

**4. The fixed points.** The dates you already know matter — your busy season, the run-up to Christmas, a local event, a quiet patch you want to fill. Mark the handful that deserve a push, and plan a little ahead of each.

That’s it. Goal, themes, rhythm, fixed points. One page. Pin it up.

## How to actually use it

Each week or month, you’re no longer inventing from scratch — you’re just asking "which theme today, and what have I got?" The plan narrows the choice, the raw material (photos, jobs, questions from customers) fills it in. Capture as you go, and the page does the thinking for you.

## Leave room for real life

The best content is often reactive — a job that went brilliantly, a question everyone suddenly asks, a bit of weather, a moment worth sharing. The one-page plan isn’t a cage; it’s a backbone. It keeps you moving when nothing’s happening and gets out of the way when something is.

## Start it before the year does

Twenty minutes with a pen now is worth more than a perfect spreadsheet you build in January and never open again. Get the page down while the year is still ahead of you.
